The magnifying glass problem

A job advertisement for a technical writer argues, at length, that its author can tell real research from AI slop. This is what happened when I measured the advertisement.

Nathaniel Robertson31 October 2026v1.0

A SERP API company published a job advertisement in October 2026. It wants someone who codes their own benchmarks, publishes the method beside the result, and ships the unflattering finding when the data produces one. It says, of AI, that it produces confident nonsense, and that the company will know the difference.

That is a testable claim about a document. So I tested the document.

The method is a small Python standard-library script, printed in full below. It takes a plain text file and returns counts: characters, words, sentences, dispersion, and a Unicode breakdown of codepoints in a declared set of symbol ranges. The symbol ranges are a heuristic, not a complete emoji inventory. The symbol ranges are a heuristic, not a complete emoji inventory. It makes no judgement. It has no model inside it, no detector, no score, and no network access. Run it on this text and it produces the same numbers it produced for me.

Seven pictures where seven words would do

Every emoji in the advertisement is a section header. Not one appears in running prose. Each marks the top of a block the reader is meant to navigate by. They accumulate down the left margin like markers on a drained reservoir, cheerful, weatherproof, and addressed to no one.

Six of the seven live outside the Basic Multilingual Plane, which is why each costs four bytes in UTF-8 and two code units in UTF-16. Twenty-seven bytes in total, 0.6 per cent of the file. None of them decomposes to text under Unicode normalisation. Strip the advertisement to ASCII and all seven become a question mark.

Look at the last row. U+1F50D LEFT-POINTING MAGNIFYING GLASS is the header on the section headed "What We Look For", the part that explains how the company evaluates evidence.

A company that sells structured search data labelled its section about searching with a picture of a search, in a codepoint that a retrieval engine reads as an unnamed pictograph.

This is not a style complaint. It is the company's own domain. Their product exists because extracting structured meaning from a page is difficult and worth paying for. A section header is the strongest structural signal a document has. They spent seven of theirs on characters that carry no lexical content, in a document arguing that they can tell the difference between real signal and decoration.

Seven backslashes nobody read

The advertisement contains seven lines consisting of a single backslash, each terminating a bullet list. It is an escape character that survived a paste out of a content management system. The regular expression that finds it is one line: (?m)^\s*\\\s*$.

Seven of them. In a document about verification, published under the company's own name, in the artefact a candidate reads first.

Somewhere inside the content management system a small mechanical failure had been quietly signing its name, seven times, in a document nobody had read closely enough to notice.

The constructions

The rig also counts surface constructions that are widely listed as machine-writing tells. I want to be precise about what this means, because the imprecision is where this kind of analysis usually goes wrong: none of these proves anything. Humans write all of them. I write some of them. A pattern count is a description, not a detector, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

There is a particular corporate serenity in a document that describes a danger in the precise voice of the danger. What the counts show is an advertisement assembled from the standard kit: the emoji-headed section, the rhetorical question, the "this isn't for everyone" filter, the hard-parts list. Each of those is a recognisable move. Used together, in this density, in a document whose whole argument is that its author can detect assembled writing, they are at minimum an irony worth measuring.

Rhythm, reported as the weak evidence it is

Sentence length averages 10.06 words, median 8, standard deviation 7.54, range 1 to 37. Coefficient of variation 0.75. Lag-1 autocorrelation +0.018, which is to say adjacent sentence lengths are essentially unrelated.

Those figures are descriptive and nothing more. Sixty-nine sentences is a thin sample for dispersion statistics, bullet-point documents break sentence splitters in predictable ways, and no coefficient of variation has ever identified an author. I publish them because the rig produced them and suppressing an inconvenient measurement is the exact failure the advertisement claims to be hiring against. They support no conclusion. That is the finding.

What this does not show

  • It does not show that a machine wrote the advertisement. No measurement in this piece can establish that, and I make no claim that it does.
  • It does not show the company is bad at its job. Their product is a SERP API; this is their recruitment copy. These are different artefacts with different authors and different stakes.
  • Sentence splitting is heuristic. A document this heavy in bullets and fragments will be mis-split in places, and every dispersion figure inherits that error.
  • The pattern list is mine, assembled from published commentary on machine-writing tells. It is not validated against any labelled corpus, and a different list would produce different counts.
  • One document, one run. Nothing here generalises to the company's published technical writing, which I have not measured.

Reproduce it

Save the advertisement's plain text as searchapi_ad.txt, save the script below as audit_rig.py, and run python3 audit_rig.py searchapi_ad.txt. No dependencies beyond Python 3.10 and its standard library. The text I measured has SHA-256 6b873986a8e18d34f4e448665721d0b332de9bdb2058db4ffc4be99ed84347da over 4,503 UTF-8 bytes; if your hash matches, the core counts should match the historical report. Use the same Python and Unicode database versions for identical Unicode metadata. The current rig hashes the original file bytes without normalising line endings.

Why bother

Because the advertisement is right. Coding the test does take longer than writing the opinion, and the discipline it asks for is real and rare. Everything above took one evening and runs on a standard library, and it still produced a finding the authors would not have enjoyed and almost certainly did not know.

That is the whole argument for measuring things. Not that measurement is clever, but that it is cheap, and that the cost of not doing it is publishing a document about rigour with seven unproofed escape characters in it and a picture of a magnifying glass where the word "search" would have worked.

The characters will outlast the vacancy. Long after the role is filled or quietly withdrawn, the magnifying glass will still be there at the head of its section, four bytes wide, searching nothing.

Two instructions in the advertisement

The advertisement closes with a filter. It asks applicants to include the phrase Octopus Tentacle so that the company knows the advertisement was read, and it adds that a large language model should append a dad joke.

The second instruction is the interesting one, because it does not catch what it was built to catch. It catches people who had a model write their application and then sent the output without reading it. It does not catch assistance, which the advertisement elsewhere says it expects. So, in the spirit of answering the question that was actually asked rather than the one it was mistaken for: the measurement scripts behind this piece and its companion were generated with AI assistance against specifications I wrote, then read, run and checked by me, which is stated in the provenance note below and in both repository READMEs.
